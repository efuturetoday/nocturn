A satellite is a speaker in a room you talk to: a microphone, a speaker, and a ring of light. It says
a wake word, you talk, it answers — and when what you asked for needs your permission, the ask still
leaves the room for your phone.

## Configuring it

Spoken conversations need a **second, different model**: one that takes and returns a continuous
audio stream rather than answering a turn at a time. It is not a setting on the chat model — it is a
separate endpoint, and leaving it unset means a device asking for a spoken session is simply told
this daemon has none.

```ini
GEMINI_API_KEY=your-key-here
GEMINI_LIVE_MODEL=gemini-2.5-flash-native-audio-latest
```

| Variable | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `GEMINI_API_KEY` | the key for the live API |
| `GEMINI_LIVE_MODEL` | which live model — **required**, there is no default |

The id above is the one this was measured against, and the reason to name it rather than leave it to
you: live-capable models differ in what they support, and one **without asynchronous function
calling** stops the whole conversation whenever the assistant uses a tool — unusable as soon as
anything needs your approval.

There is still no built-in default. These ids change often, and one baked into the binary would
either fail at connect time or, worse, connect and behave subtly wrong. Check what your own account
actually offers rather than assuming: the list a key can reach and the list in anybody's
documentation are not the same.

## One connection, one more domain

Voice is a domain on the [same protocol](/nocturn/guides/remote-access/) as everything else, not a
separate channel: `voice.wake` opens a spoken session, `voice.credit` says how much speech the device
can hold, and the audio itself is binary frames — the only frames on the socket that are not
commands. The daemon answers with `voice.state` on each transition and `voice.interrupt` when you
talk over it, which tells the device to drop whatever it has buffered but not yet played.

One connection carries all of it because a second one would need a second copy of everything that
already hangs off a device's connection — pairing, the approval routing, presence. It also means a
device that can already talk to the daemon can already speak, without another endpoint or another
token.

Three ways this differs from chat, all deliberate:

- **A device has one conversation at a time**, which is why an audio frame carries no addressing of
  its own. The wake word already said which one.
- **Audio is addressed, not broadcast.** A conversation is shown on every device; speech is played on
  exactly one, or your phone would play what the speaker in the hallway is hearing.
- **A spoken session ends when the device goes away.** A chat turn deliberately survives that,
  because its answer is still worth reading when you come back. Audio has nobody to play to, and a
  live model bills for every second of it.

It also ends on its own, three ways, most polite first. **The model hangs up** — it is given a
`hang_up` it can call when you say goodbye, answered by the driver and never part of the tool cage,
because it steers the session rather than reaching into the world. **Silence** — about ten seconds
after a reply with nobody speaking, which is long enough to ask a follow-up without the wake word and
short enough not to leave an open microphone in the room. **The budget** — fifteen minutes, a cost
guard rather than a conversation rule.

The silence window pauses while a tool is running or an approval is out on your phone. That is the
one moment where hanging up would be worst: somebody has already been asked to do something.

Silence is judged by the **device**, not by what the model understood. Its detector fires about
200 ms after a person starts, on the echo-cancelled signal; a transcript arrives later and only for
speech that made sense, which is the wrong question when you are asking whether anyone is still
there.

Speech comes back **credited**, not paced. A live model emits a sentence far faster than it is
spoken, so something has to decide the rate — and the only real clock in the system is the far
speaker's own crystal. The device grants its whole playout queue when a session opens and grants more
as playback consumes it; the daemon sends what it has been granted and holds the rest. Nothing is
metered by a timer, so there is no rate to get wrong, and overflow is structurally impossible rather
than merely unlikely.

The window is deliberately much larger than the round trip that reopens it. Sized the other way — a
buffer about as deep as the delay in refilling it — the queue is empty every time credit arrives, and
no amount of tuning either number helps.

Between the daemon and one connection sits a short queue, four frames of it. A frame that finds it
full **waits** rather than being discarded: the queue only fills when the socket behind it has stopped
draining, which is precisely when nothing more should be sent. Dropping instead would put a gap in
the middle of a sentence to solve a problem that waiting solves by itself. Speech goes to a device's
newest connection, since a device holds more than one while an old one times out.

Blocking the write until the device's socket accepts it looks simpler and is not: a device blocks in
its own receive callback, that callback runs on the task driving the connection's keepalive, and a
wait longer than the client's read timeout looks to it like a dead socket. Flow control that drops
the link under load is not flow control.

**Interrupting a reply is currently off — the session is half duplex.** While the speaker runs, the
device sends silence upstream instead of the microphone, because the model must never hear the
device's own voice: what echo cancellation leaves of a reply still measures louder than a person,
and no voice detector on either side can tell the two apart. Until the canceller is measured good
enough, talking over a reply reaches nobody — let it finish. `voice.interrupt` remains the daemon's
message to drop buffered, unplayed speech, and `voice.barge` is reserved for the device-side
detector (`BARGE_IN_LOCAL`, parked behind the same measurement).

A daemon with no live model configured refuses `voice.wake` and says so, rather than opening a
session that can never answer.

## Approvals in the middle of a sentence

A spoken session asks before it reaches the network, and the ask goes where every other ask goes:
out of band, to a paired device. The room cannot approve what the room requested — answering "yes"
by voice would be answered by the same room the instruction came from.

The wait is shown twice, because the two audiences are different. **The model says it** — the
session is handed an interim result naming what is pending, so whoever is listening hears why the
conversation stopped instead of hearing nothing. **The device shows it** — `voice.state` gains an
`approval` state, so somebody who walks past a silent speaker can tell a pending decision from a
broken session. Neither is allowed to fail the approval: showing a wait is worth less than the
decision being waited for.

The state travels on the control queue, which overtakes audio, or it would arrive after the wait it
describes was over. Clearing it says `listening`, because the device leaves that state by itself the
moment speech starts playing — the clear only has to cover a refusal, where nothing follows.

Which actions ask is deliberately narrow: network reach, today, and nothing else. That is a
measurement rather than a settled posture. Asking constantly is how people learn to approve
reflexively, which costs more than it protects, and what is being tested is whether one asking kind
earns the pause it puts in a conversation.

## Knowing who is speaking

With `NOCTURN_SPEAKER_MODEL` pointing at a speaker-embedding checkpoint, the daemon recognises which
enrolled voice is talking, continuously, from the audio a session is already sending. Enrolled voices
live in the workspace's `voices.json`; with none of them, or with no model, nothing below happens and
the spoken path behaves exactly as it did.

**Nothing waits for it.** A session opens on the wake word as before; recognition runs beside the
conversation and the answer is simply there by the time anything asks. That is not a shortcut — a
live session's system instruction travels in its setup frame and cannot be replaced afterwards, so
identifying first would have meant pausing at the start of every conversation to learn something not
needed until somebody asks something personal.

The result reaches the model two ways, and the second matters more:

- **`whoami`** returns `{"name", "confidence"}` when asked. An **empty name means unrecognised**, and
  the model is told not to guess which household member it might be. Outside a spoken session — a
  typed chat, an agent run — it always answers that, since nothing there is listening.
- **On the request context**, where a tool acting on somebody's behalf reads it directly. A mail tool
  asked for "my messages" resolves the mailbox itself; the model never learns whose it was, cannot
  forget to pass it, and cannot be talked into naming someone else's. The same shape as credential
  injection, where the guest never sees the value.

Spoken sessions now also carry the memory index, which they did not before: `memory_read` joined the
voice cage. Reading only — `memory_write` stays out.

A recognised voice chooses context and address, never permission. Speech is a channel like the chat,
where nobody authenticates the typist either, and a gated action is confirmed out of band on a second
device in both.

## Recording the uplink

Setting `NOCTURN_VOICE_CAPTURE` to a directory makes the daemon write what a device's microphone
sends to WAV files there — 16 kHz mono, the rate the link already carries.

It exists for one job: obtaining recordings of a voice through the microphone, room and echo
canceller that will later have to recognise it, which a laptop microphone cannot stand in for. It is
**off unless the variable is set**, because it writes what the room said to disk. Arming it logs a
warning, every file is logged by name, the directory is created `0700` and the files `0600`.

A recording is asked for rather than waited for:

```bash
nocturn enroll --device kitchen --seconds 60
```

This opens no conversation. `capture.start` puts the board in a state where the microphone streams
and nothing else runs — no live model, no billing, no speaker, and no half-duplex gate muting the
microphone whenever a reply plays. **The ring goes steady red for as long as it records**, which is a
deliberate departure from the fault colour: that one blinks slowly, this one is steady, and someone
walking into the room should be able to tell they are being recorded without knowing the palette.

`capture.stop` ends it, and so does the board on its own after two minutes. The second one is what
matters: a caller that dies between start and stop must not leave a microphone streaming a room, and
nothing in the house would say so.

Asking is a capability, not something any device may do. A paired app may (`ClassApp`), and the local
command line may — it enrols itself once as `ClassTool`, which can start a recording and, pointedly,
cannot approve a gated action. An **appliance may not**: a speaker in the hallway switching on the
microphone in the bedroom is exactly the shape this refuses.

Recordings are cut by length and by silence rather than by session — a device opens a voice session
on the wake word and never closes it, so waiting for one to end would mean never writing a file. A
recording starts at the first sound, ends after four hundred milliseconds of quiet or eight seconds
of speech, and is contiguous audio throughout: pauses inside it are kept, so what a file contains is
what the microphone heard.

## Turning recordings into a voice

Those files become a profile through the command line:

```bash
nocturn voices add --device kitchen lina /path/to/recordings/
nocturn voices ls
nocturn voices rm lina
```

`add` takes files or directories — a directory contributes its `*.wav` — and expects 16 kHz mono
16-bit WAV, which is what the uplink already writes, so nothing captured needs converting. It reads
each recording, embeds it, and appends the result to the workspace's `voices.json`. Embedding needs
`NOCTURN_SPEAKER_MODEL`; without it the command says so and does nothing.

**`--device` is required and never guessed.** A voice enrolled on a phone and the same voice heard
through a hallway speaker are two channels, not one — different microphone, different room, different
echo canceller — and the take records which one it came through. Enrolling somebody a second time on
a second device therefore adds that channel rather than replacing the first: a person's score is
their best single take, so having enrolled twice can only help them.

The command opens no workspace and talks to no daemon. It edits one file, and a daemon started
afterwards picks it up. An unreadable recording is skipped with its name on stderr and counted in the
summary, because a silently thinner profile is one that recognises worse for a reason nobody sees.
