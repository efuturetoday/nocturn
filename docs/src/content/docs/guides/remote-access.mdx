The terminal chat is one way in. The other is the **daemon**: one command turns a workspace into
something your phone can talk to over your own network.

```bash
nocturn serve            # listens on :8080 by default, or $NOCTURN_ADDR
```

The daemon is not a cloud service and does not phone home. It listens on your LAN, announces itself
over Bonjour/DNS-SD as `_nocturn._tcp` so the companion app can find it without you typing an IP,
and speaks a small tagged-JSON protocol over one WebSocket per device.

## Pairing a device <Badge text="Available now" variant="success" />

Every connection is gated on a bearer token, and the daemon never stores the token itself — only its
SHA-256 hash, compared in constant time. Getting a device its token happens in one of three ways.

**The first device** redeems a bootstrap code that the daemon prints at startup (`POST /pair`). That
is the only time a code comes from the machine itself.

**Every device after that** asks an already-paired one for permission:

1. the new device posts a join request (`POST /join`) and gets back a `joinId` — never a code;
2. the request appears **live** on every connected paired device, which is the only place the code
   is ever shown;
3. you read the code on the trusted device and enter it on the new one (`POST /join/confirm`), which
   redeems it for that device's own bearer.

The property that buys: a device that is merely on your network cannot join. It needs a human at an
already-trusted device to hand it a code.

**A device that cannot read a code** is enrolled *for* — a speaker in a hallway has no display to
show one and no keyboard to enter one, so the exchange above has nothing to work with. An
already-paired device asks the daemon to mint a bearer on its behalf (`POST /devices`) and hands it
over. Letting such a device enrol itself is not the alternative: anything that can enrol itself is
not being authorised by anyone.

## What a device is, and what that lets it do

Every device carries a **class**, which is a fact about the device rather than a list of permissions.
What a class may do is decided in one place, so the rules can change without rewriting what is
already stored.

| Class | May answer approvals | May enrol devices |
|---|---|---|
| `app` | yes | yes |
| `appliance` | no | no |

An **appliance** is anything with no authenticated input path — a voice satellite, a doorbell, a car.
Whoever is present can operate it and none of them identified themselves, so nothing it reports is
anybody's consent. It is not shown approvals at all: a connection that may not answer one is never
attached to the approval broker, so there is nothing for it to answer *with*.

Enrolment follows one rule rather than a list of exceptions: **a device may only enrol a class whose
abilities its own class already covers.** An appliance therefore enrols nothing, so a stolen
appliance token cannot multiply into a household of equally trusted microphones.

## What the app can do

The companion app talks to the same workspace the terminal does — same conversations, same agents,
same grants. The protocol is one tagged message per action (`chat.submit`, `chat.open`,
`agent.fire`, `approval.resolve`, `reminder.list`, …), and everything the assistant produces streams
back the same way (`chat.token`, `chat.thinking`, `chat.tool`, `approval.request`).

Two consequences worth knowing:

- **Conversations are not per device.** Start something in the terminal, pick it up on your phone.
- **The first answer wins.** If two devices are shown an approval, whichever answers first resolves
  it; the other is told it was resolved. This is also why an appliance is never shown one — it would
  outrace the phone it exists to defer to.

## Speaking, on the same connection

Voice is a domain on the protocol above, not a separate channel: `voice.wake` opens a spoken session,
`voice.credit` says how much speech the device can hold, and the audio itself is binary frames — the
only frames on the socket that are not commands. The daemon answers with `voice.state` on each
transition and `voice.interrupt` when you talk over it, which tells the device to drop whatever it
has buffered but not yet played.

One connection carries all of it because a second one would need a second copy of everything that
already hangs off a device's connection — pairing, the approval routing, presence. It also means a
device that can already talk to the daemon can already speak, without another endpoint or another
token.

Three ways this differs from chat, all deliberate:

- **A device has one conversation at a time**, which is why an audio frame carries no addressing of
  its own. The wake word already said which one.
- **Audio is addressed, not broadcast.** A conversation is shown on every device; speech is played on
  exactly one, or your phone would play what the speaker in the hallway is hearing.
- **A spoken session ends when the device goes away.** A chat turn deliberately survives that,
  because its answer is still worth reading when you come back. Audio has nobody to play to, and a
  live model bills for every second of it.

It also ends on its own, three ways, most polite first. **The model hangs up** — it is given a
`hang_up` it can call when you say goodbye, answered by the driver and never part of the tool cage,
because it steers the session rather than reaching into the world. **Silence** — about ten seconds
after a reply with nobody speaking, which is long enough to ask a follow-up without the wake word and
short enough not to leave an open microphone in the room. **The budget** — fifteen minutes, a cost
guard rather than a conversation rule.

The silence window pauses while a tool is running or an approval is out on your phone. That is the
one moment where hanging up would be worst: somebody has already been asked to do something.

Silence is judged by the **device**, not by what the model understood. Its detector fires about
200 ms after a person starts, on the echo-cancelled signal; a transcript arrives later and only for
speech that made sense, which is the wrong question when you are asking whether anyone is still
there.

Speech comes back **credited**, not paced. A live model emits a sentence far faster than it is
spoken, so something has to decide the rate — and the only real clock in the system is the far
speaker's own crystal. The device grants its whole playout queue when a session opens and grants more
as playback consumes it; the daemon sends what it has been granted and holds the rest. Nothing is
metered by a timer, so there is no rate to get wrong, and overflow is structurally impossible rather
than merely unlikely.

The window is deliberately much larger than the round trip that reopens it. Sized the other way — a
buffer about as deep as the delay in refilling it — the queue is empty every time credit arrives, and
no amount of tuning either number helps.

Between the daemon and one connection sits a short queue, four frames of it. A frame that finds it
full **waits** rather than being discarded: the queue only fills when the socket behind it has stopped
draining, which is precisely when nothing more should be sent. Dropping instead would put a gap in
the middle of a sentence to solve a problem that waiting solves by itself. Speech goes to a device's
newest connection, since a device holds more than one while an old one times out.

Blocking the write until the device's socket accepts it looks simpler and is not: a device blocks in
its own receive callback, that callback runs on the task driving the connection's keepalive, and a
wait longer than the client's read timeout looks to it like a dead socket. Flow control that drops
the link under load is not flow control.

**Interrupting a reply is currently off — the session is half duplex.** While the speaker runs, the
device sends silence upstream instead of the microphone, because the model must never hear the
device's own voice: what echo cancellation leaves of a reply still measures louder than a person,
and no voice detector on either side can tell the two apart. Until the canceller is measured good
enough, talking over a reply reaches nobody — let it finish. `voice.interrupt` remains the daemon's
message to drop buffered, unplayed speech, and `voice.barge` is reserved for the device-side
detector (`BARGE_IN_LOCAL`, parked behind the same measurement).

A daemon with no live model configured refuses `voice.wake` and says so, rather than opening a
session that can never answer.

## Approvals when nobody is looking <Badge text="Available now" variant="success" />

This is the reason the daemon exists. An agent running on a schedule hits something that needs your
yes, with no terminal in front of it. If a device is paired, the ask is routed out of band: a push
wakes your phone, you approve or deny in the app, and the agent continues or stops.

The push carries **no authority**. It is a wake signal and nothing else — the decision travels back
over the authenticated WebSocket, never in the notification. A notification that arrives twice, or
gets replayed, cannot approve anything.

With no paired device, an agent set to `guarded` behaves as `strict`: the ask is denied and the run
reports why. Missing setup reduces authority; it never grants it. See
[Approvals](/guides/approvals/).

## Not there yet

**Messaging channels** <Badge text="Planned" variant="caution" /> — starting and steering a
workspace through WhatsApp or Telegram, with approvals in the same conversation.

**A public HTTP API** <Badge text="Planned" variant="caution" /> — the WebSocket protocol is
internal to the app today; a documented, stable surface for your own tools is not something to build
against yet.
